<Part id="why">
  No company exists alone. A great business in a shrinking industry can still struggle, and an average one in a booming industry can do well. Judges want to see that you understand the world your company competes in, not just the company.

  This module also answers the most important question in investing: what stops competitors from taking this company's customers? If you can answer that clearly, you've found the heart of your pitch.
</Part>

<Part id="learn">
  ### Describe the industry

  Start with the big picture. Answer three questions:

  1. **How big is the industry, and is it growing or shrinking?** Give a number and a source.
  2. **What trends are changing it?** Think about technology, how customers shop, prices, rules and laws, and who's entering or leaving.
  3. **What makes a company win in this industry?** Lowest price? Best product? Biggest network? Strongest brand?

  Good sources: the Competition section of your company's <GlossaryTerm id="10-k">10-K</GlossaryTerm>, its competitors' 10-Ks, government statistics like the US Census Bureau's retail data for retail companies, and industry coverage in reliable news outlets. Avoid paid "market research" summaries with no clear source.

  ### Name the competitors

  List three to five main competitors, and include companies that compete for the same customer in a different way. A streaming service competes with other streaming services, but also with video games and social media for people's free time.

  For each competitor, note how it competes differently: cheaper, bigger, more specialized, or online instead of in stores.

  ### Competitive advantage: the "moat"

  A <GlossaryTerm id="moat">moat</GlossaryTerm> is something that protects a company's profits from competitors, the way a moat protects a castle. These are the five most common kinds:

  <div class="overflow-x-auto" role="region" aria-label="The five most common kinds of moat" tabindex="0">

  | Moat | What it means | Example question to ask |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Brand | Customers trust it or want it, and will pay more | Would customers switch if a rival were slightly cheaper? |
  | Scale | Being bigger makes it cheaper to operate | Can a smaller rival match its prices and still profit? |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="network-effects">Network effects</GlossaryTerm> | The product gets better as more people use it | Does each new user make it more valuable for others? |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="switching-costs">Switching costs</GlossaryTerm> | It's expensive or annoying for customers to leave | What would a customer lose by switching? |
  | Cost advantage | It can make or deliver things more cheaply than anyone else | Why are its costs lower, and can that be copied? |

  </div>

  Most strong companies have one or two moats, not five. Don't claim a moat without evidence. A number, like a high renewal rate or a falling cost per unit, beats an adjective every time.

  ### What goes in your Industry Overview and Competitive Positioning

  About one page:

  - The industry's size, growth, and two or three key trends, with sources
  - The main competitors and how each one competes
  - Where your company fits: what it does better or worse than rivals
  - Its moat, with evidence, or an honest note that it doesn't have a strong one
</Part>

  ### A sample paragraph on competitive positioning

  > Costco's main advantage is scale. With $297.2 billion in net sales in fiscal 2026, it can buy in enormous volumes and still profit while keeping its gross margin at just 11.09% of net sales, which its 10-K describes as "significantly lower" than most other retailers. Membership fees reinforce this: members who have paid a yearly fee have a reason to keep coming back, and 92.3% of members in the US and Canada renewed in fiscal 2026. The biggest competitive threats are Sam's Club, which copies the same model, and online retailers like Amazon. Costco's 10-K also warns that AI shopping tools could change how people shop and help rivals compete.

  Notice the pattern: a claim, then a number that proves it, then a source. It also names the threats honestly, instead of pretending the company has none.

<Part id="do">
  1. **Read the Competition part of your company's 10-K** (usually in Item 1). Write down every competitor it names.
  2. **Find one number for the industry's size and one for its growth**, each with a source and date.
  3. **List two or three trends** changing the industry.
  4. **Build a competitor table** like the one in See it, with three to five rivals and how each competes.
  5. **Choose your company's moat**, or decide it doesn't have a strong one. Find at least one number as evidence.
  6. **Draft your Industry Overview and Competitive Positioning section**, about a page, with a source for every number.
  7. **Tick off module 3** in your [progress tracker](/learn#progress) and share the draft with your mentor.

  **Deliverable:** your Industry Overview and Competitive Positioning draft. Due by <time datetime={new Date(frontmatter.due).toISOString().slice(0, 10)}>{formatDue(new Date(frontmatter.due))}</time>.
</Part>

<Part id="check">
  1. Does your draft give the industry's size and growth, with sources?
  2. Have you named at least three competitors, and how each one competes?
  3. Is your company's moat backed by a number, not just an adjective?
  4. Have you named at least one real competitive threat?
  5. Could a judge tell, from this section alone, why customers choose your company over its rivals?

  **Common mistakes:** listing competitors without comparing them, claiming a moat with no evidence, using an industry number with no source or date, and ignoring new kinds of competitors, like online rivals for a store-based business.
</Part>
