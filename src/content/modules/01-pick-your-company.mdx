<Part id="why">
  You're going to spend the next two months with this company. You'll read its reports, learn its competitors, and explain it on camera. Pick a business you find interesting and can actually understand, and every step after this gets easier. Pick one that's confusing or has little information out there, and you'll be fighting it the whole way.

  Your choice also shapes your score. Judges reward real insight into the company and its industry, and it's much easier to say something insightful about a business you understand. Getting the pick right is the best head start you can give yourself.
</Part>

<Part id="learn">
  ### What "publicly traded" means

  A publicly traded company is one whose shares anyone can buy and sell on a stock <GlossaryTerm id="exchange">exchange</GlossaryTerm>, like the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq. Each one has a <GlossaryTerm id="ticker">ticker</GlossaryTerm>, a short code for its stock: Apple's is AAPL. In the United States, public companies also have to publish detailed reports about their business, including an annual report called a 10-K. Those reports will be your best source of information, and they're free for anyone to read.

  ### What makes a good first pick

  Run every company you're considering through these five questions:

  1. **Can you explain how it makes money in two sentences?** If you can't, keep looking. You'll have to explain it to judges in a few minutes.
  2. **Is there plenty of public information?** Look for annual reports, earnings calls, and regular news coverage. Bigger, well-known companies usually have more.
  3. **Is it profitable?** Your valuation in module 5 uses the <GlossaryTerm id="pe-ratio">price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio</GlossaryTerm>, which only works when a company has positive <GlossaryTerm id="earnings">earnings</GlossaryTerm>. A quick check: if a market data site shows the P/E as "N/A" or negative, choose another company.
  4. **Is there something to debate?** The best pitches answer a question people disagree about: Can this company keep growing? Is its stock too expensive? Will a new competitor hurt it? A company where everyone agrees on everything is hard to pitch.
  5. **Do you actually care about it?** Two months is a long time. Interest keeps you going in January.

  ### What to avoid on a first pitch

  Some companies are much harder for beginners, even though they're allowed:

  - **Banks and insurance companies.** They make money in unusual ways, and they're valued differently from most businesses.
  - **Early-stage biotech companies.** Many have no approved products and no profits, so P/E doesn't work.
  - **Companies that are being bought.** When a deal is announced, the stock price mostly follows the deal, not the business.
  - **Very small companies.** They often have little news coverage and sparse information.

  ### Buy or sell?

  You don't need to decide yet, and you shouldn't. Choose a company you want to understand, and let your research tell you whether to recommend buying or selling. Both are valid, and a well-argued sell can stand out. Deciding first and then hunting for evidence is how pitches end up one-sided.

  Also, a stock going up a lot recently isn't a reason to pick it. You're judging the business, not last month's price chart.

  ### Where to find ideas

  - **Things you use.** The apps on your phone, where you shop, what you eat, what you watch.
  - **The [Stock Matchmaker](/tools/matchmaker)**, which matches your interests to public companies.
  - **The news.** A company in the headlines often has exactly the kind of debate that makes a good pitch.
  - **An industry you're curious about.** Pick the industry first, then compare its biggest companies.

  ### One team per company

  In GHIC, no two teams pitch the same company, so every pitch is different. Once you and your partner agree, claim your company on the <a href={claimBoardUrl}>claim board</a>. If someone already has it, pick another or ask that team whether they need a partner.
</Part>

  ### A sample "why this company" paragraph

  > I want to pitch Costco because almost everyone I know shops there, but I don't understand how a store that keeps its prices so low makes money. I've read that membership fees are a big part of the answer. The question I want to answer is whether Costco can keep growing fast enough to justify what investors are paying for its stock today.

  Notice what the paragraph does: it says what the company is, why you're curious, and one real question to research. It doesn't say buy or sell, because that comes after the research.

<Part id="do">
  1. **Make a shortlist of three companies.** Use the ideas above or the [Stock Matchmaker](/tools/matchmaker).
  2. **Run each one through the five questions.** The [fit check tool](/tools/fit-check) on this site walks you through them. Cross off any company that fails questions 1–3.
  3. **Pick one with your partner.** Check the claim board first; if it's open, claim it under both your names.
  4. **Find its latest annual report.** Search for the company on SEC EDGAR, the government's free database of company filings, and open its most recent 10-K. You don't need to read it yet; just make sure you can find it.
  5. **Write one paragraph: why this company?** Say what the company does, why you're interested, and one real question you want to answer. Don't pick buy or sell yet.
  6. **Send your ticker and paragraph to your mentor**, or bring them to the first check-in, and tick off module 1 in your [progress tracker](/learn#progress).

  **Deliverable:** your claimed ticker and a one-paragraph "why this company." Due by <time datetime={new Date(frontmatter.due).toISOString().slice(0, 10)}>{formatDue(new Date(frontmatter.due))}</time>.
</Part>

<Part id="check">
  Before moving on, make sure you can say yes to each of these:

  1. Can you explain how your company makes money in two sentences, without notes?
  2. Does it have positive earnings, so P/E works?
  3. Is it claimed on the claim board under your names?
  4. Have you found its latest 10-K?
  5. Does your paragraph end with a real question, not a buy or sell decision?

  If any answer is no, fix that before your first check-in.

  **Common mistakes:** picking a stock because its price went up a lot recently, deciding buy or sell before doing any research, choosing a company that's being bought by another company, and picking a business so complex you can't explain it.
</Part>
