<Part id="why">
  Numbers turn your story into evidence. Saying "the company is growing" is an opinion; saying "revenue grew 10% last year" is a fact a judge can check. Sound financial analysis is worth points in both the report and the presentation.

  You don't need to be a math expert. You need a handful of numbers, the trend in each one, and a sentence about what that trend means. This module shows you which numbers matter and how to read them.
</Part>

<Part id="learn">
  ### Where the numbers live

  The financial statements are in Item 8 of the <GlossaryTerm id="10-k">10-K</GlossaryTerm>. Most companies also post an <GlossaryTerm id="earnings-release">earnings press release</GlossaryTerm> each quarter with the key numbers on the first page. Always use at least three years so you can see a trend; the 10-K shows two or three years side by side.

  ### The income statement: five lines that matter most

  The <GlossaryTerm id="income-statement">income statement</GlossaryTerm> shows money coming in and going out over a year, from the top line (revenue) to the bottom line (profit).

  <div class="overflow-x-auto" role="region" aria-label="Five income statement lines that matter most" tabindex="0">

  | Line | What it tells you |
  | --- | --- |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="revenue">Revenue</GlossaryTerm> | Everything the company earned from customers. Also called sales or the "top line." |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="gross-margin">Gross margin</GlossaryTerm> | What's left after the direct cost of the products sold, as a percent of revenue. Shows pricing power. |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="operating-income">Operating income</GlossaryTerm> | Profit from running the business, before interest and taxes. |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="net-income">Net income</GlossaryTerm> | Profit after everything, including interest and taxes. The "bottom line." |
  | <GlossaryTerm id="eps">Earnings per share (EPS)</GlossaryTerm> | Net income divided by the number of shares. This is the number your valuation uses in module 5. |

  </div>

  Use <GlossaryTerm id="diluted-eps">diluted EPS</GlossaryTerm>, which counts shares that could be created from things like stock options. It's the standard figure analysts use.

  ### Two calculations you'll use constantly

  **Growth rate:** how much something changed from one year to the next.

  $$
  \text{Growth} = \frac{\text{This year} - \text{Last year}}{\text{Last year}}
  $$

  **Margin:** how much of each dollar of revenue becomes profit.

  $$
  \text{Operating margin} = \frac{\text{Operating income}}{\text{Revenue}}
  $$

  A company can grow revenue while its margins shrink, which means it's earning less on each sale. Always look at both.

  Some companies define a figure in their own way. Read the label carefully, and if your company calculates something differently from the standard, say so.

  ### The balance sheet: can it survive a bad year?

  The <GlossaryTerm id="balance-sheet">balance sheet</GlossaryTerm> is a snapshot of what the company owns and owes on one day. For a first pitch, check three things:

  - **Cash:** money available right now
  - **Debt:** money it has borrowed and must repay
  - **Cash compared with debt:** more cash than debt means a company can handle a rough patch; lots of debt and little cash is a risk worth naming

  ### Turning numbers into observations

  A table of numbers isn't analysis. For each important trend, write one sentence that says what happened, by how much, and why it matters. Use Item 7 (<GlossaryTerm id="mdna">Management's Discussion and Analysis</GlossaryTerm>) to find out why the numbers changed.
</Part>

<Part id="see">
  ### Costco's key financials, fiscal 2024 to 2026

  Costco's <GlossaryTerm id="fiscal-year">fiscal year</GlossaryTerm> ends on the Sunday nearest the end of August; fiscal 2026 ran from September 2025 to August 30, 2026. Dollar amounts are in billions, except EPS.

  <div class="overflow-x-auto" role="region" aria-label="Costco income statement figures, fiscal 2024 to 2026" tabindex="0">

  | | Fiscal 2024 | Fiscal 2025 | Fiscal 2026 |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Total revenue | $254.5B | $275.2B | $303.2B |
  | Revenue growth | | 8.2% | 10.1% |
  | Membership fees | $4.8B | $5.3B | $5.9B |
  | Gross margin (% of net sales) | 10.92% | 11.12% | 11.09% |
  | Operating income | $9.3B | $10.4B | $11.7B |
  | Operating margin | 3.6% | 3.8% | 3.9% |
  | Net income | $7.4B | $8.1B | $9.2B |
  | Diluted EPS | $16.56 | $18.21 | $20.76 |
  | EPS growth | | 10.0% | 14.0% |

  </div>

  <div class="overflow-x-auto" role="region" aria-label="Costco balance sheet figures" tabindex="0">

  | Balance sheet | Aug 31, 2025 | Aug 30, 2026 |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Cash and cash equivalents | $14.2B | $20.2B |
  | Total debt | $5.8B | $6.2B |

  </div>

  Source: Costco 10-K for fiscal 2026. Total revenue is <GlossaryTerm id="net-sales">net sales</GlossaryTerm> plus membership fees. Costco calculates gross margin on net sales only, not total revenue, so that row follows the company's own definition.

  ### Three sample observations

  > 1. **Growth sped up.** Total revenue grew 10.1% in fiscal 2026, faster than the 8.2% growth the year before.
  > 2. **Profit grew faster than sales.** Diluted EPS rose 14.0% while revenue rose 10.1%, and operating margin edged up from 3.6% in fiscal 2024 to 3.9% in fiscal 2026. Costco is keeping a little more of each dollar it earns.
  > 3. **Membership fees punch above their weight.** Fees were only about 2% of total revenue, but at $5.9 billion they equaled about half of Costco's $11.7 billion operating income. Because Costco sells goods at such thin margins, fee income does a lot to support its profits.

  Notice that each observation gives a number, a comparison, and what it means. The third one is the kind of insight judges look for: something you only see by putting two numbers side by side.

  5. **Write three observations**, each with a number, a comparison, and what it means.
